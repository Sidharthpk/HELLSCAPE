# HELLSCAPE — The Last Witness

**Complete proposed story canon and implementation brief · 4 October 2026**

This is a replacement narrative for the existing game, not a change to its gameplay code. It preserves the delivery prologue, apartment murder, rooftop fall, office puzzles, both chases, Elias fight and mercy choice, flood and rescue outcomes, van escape, vehicle boss fight, and two ending structures. Dialogue, notes, some shot compositions, and a few short character beats change. New playable locations, combat systems, inventory systems, and playable flashbacks are not required.

The proposal has been checked against the current branches and their edge cases. It is a defined canon to implement and test, not a claim that any future presentation of it will be incapable of creating ambiguity.

## The story in one page

You are a delivery driver who has spent his life looking after his younger brother, Derek. Three weeks ago, Derek came home with blood on his clothes and said he had hurt a cleaner during a fight. You helped rewrite his account as a workplace accident. Your employer, Arthur Kessler, assured you that the cleaner had walked away. You chose to believe him.

Tonight is your last ordinary shift. You deliver a parcel to Mrs. Okafor and a sealed cool box to a clinic. Derek has promised breakfast. At home you find his body. His bedroom camera shows Elias Rourke stabbing him. You pursue Elias to the roof, push him over, and fall with him.

You wake in a red reflection of the city. The dead from your workplace are here. So is the cleaner: Tomas Rourke, Elias's brother. In the office, the evidence establishes that Derek did not merely hurt Tomas. Kessler ordered him to kill the man who discovered their trade in human remains. Your carefully worded accident report helped bury the truth. The handwriting you have been following is your own.

But Derek's last three weeks were not simply fear of being caught. He was collecting evidence against the operation, including evidence against himself. The parcel you delivered to Mrs. Okafor contains his signed confession and copies of the records. His final delivery was an attempt to tell the truth without asking you to cover for him again. You completed it before knowing what it was.

Maya, who first reported Tomas to Kessler, discovered Derek's plan. She supplied Elias with enough genuine evidence to direct his revenge, while concealing the confession already on its way out. She intended him to kill Kessler and Derek, then be caught or killed himself. Instead, Elias murdered several of her colleagues as well. She and Sam fled to the silo and died when its damaged walkway collapsed.

Below, Kessler and Maya make different uses of the same terrible offer: the ruler of this city will lend the dead a monstrous body in exchange for ownership of it. Confession itself transforms nobody. Kessler buys power rather than face a hearing. Maya has purchased the same protection in advance. If you spare Elias, she calls on it to silence the truth herself; Elias holds her off while you escape. If you execute him, she hides her bargain and asks you to save her. If she subsequently drowns, the bargain collects her. Sam made no bargain; drowning leaves him a helpless ghost.

The escape forces you to carry people instead of sealed cargo. At the bridge, Kessler tries to destroy the witnesses who survived him. You defeat his swollen, borrowed body; the drowned reclaim it. The King then weighs your own conduct, including the rooftop killing. He does not tell you that Derek was innocent or that your successful delivery cancelled your crimes.

Derek has already reached the bridge and accepted a separate sentence. His fate is settled for this chapter: beyond the fog, alive only as a soul, serving judgement. You cannot kill or bargain your way into undoing it.

If your actions show that you continue to make other people expendable, you become one of the city's hunters. Otherwise you receive a sentence of restitution: return as a marked witness and recover the names and accounts Kessler erased. The companion cases are addressed separately. Your last act is to walk back into the city with your own name finally attached to the truth.

The first delivery was something your brother trusted you to carry. The last is a truth you would once have destroyed for him.

## The dramatic foundation

The emotional subject is protective love becoming control. The protagonist, Derek, Elias, Maya, and Kessler all claim to act for somebody else. Their actions gradually show when that claim is care, when it is fear, and when it is an excuse.

The protagonist's initial objective is to punish Derek's killer. His later objective is to survive long enough to tell the whole account, including the parts that condemn Derek and himself. Neither brother is secretly innocent. Elias's grief is legitimate; his murders are not justified. Maya can be worth rescuing without being trustworthy. Tomas does not exist to forgive everyone.

There are three connected reversals rather than a succession of unrelated secret identities:

1. The murdered brother was also a murderer. The CCTV is accurate.
2. The protagonist's protection helped keep Derek trapped in Kessler's control. The accident report is his deliberate work, not an unread signature.
3. The routine parcel was Derek's confession. While the protagonist was preparing to keep protecting him, Derek had finally arranged to be held responsible.

The third reversal must be established before the kill-or-spare choice. Killing Elias must never lock the player out of the central explanation.

## Fixed rules of the red city

These rules govern every branch. Characters may misunderstand them, but the story must identify mistaken explanations and provide observable correction before a consequential choice.

**1. First death brings a person into the city in a temporary human body.** This is an afterlife, not a dream, a simulation, a memory loop, or a test that secretly never happened. Physical wounds can heal enough for the person to move; this does not mean the living person survived. Maya and Sam must explicitly discover that they died in the collapse.

**2. Losing that body normally leaves a Lost soul.** The Lost are blue, diminished ghosts, not a class of people whose sins are secret. Innocent Tomas and guilty office workers can both be Lost. They retain memory and speech but cannot resume an ordinary journey in a solid body. They are not erased from existence. A ghost can briefly exert force at a place of strong personal attachment, at the cost of exhausting its manifestation. This is shown by Tomas holding Kessler at the silo approach and then fading. It is not an unlimited ability to fight, drive, or carry everyone to safety.

**3. The King offers power before judgement at an explicit price.** A soul may accept a borrowed monstrous body now, or reserve it to activate when its current body fails. The reservation can also be invoked early. Both forms surrender control of the resulting body to the King. The person understands the cost and accepts it; ordinary employee paperwork, frightened exclamations, and accidental words do not constitute agreement. A bound person can keep their human appearance until invocation or bodily death.

**4. Confession is evidence, not a magical trigger.** An admission neither damns nor absolves someone automatically. Kessler transforms because he accepts the offer of a body. Maya transforms because she invokes her existing bargain, or because its stated death condition occurs. Elias, the protagonist, and the ghosts may admit wrongdoing without changing shape.

**5. Judgement and early bargains are different routes to damnation.** The King can sentence an unbound soul at the bridge; he cannot impose that final sentence on a person still inside the city. A willing bargain lets someone obtain the sentence's power early. His jurisdiction concerns the dead. He cannot alter a living delivery, make a human investigation succeed, restore Derek to life, or manufacture a witness's voluntary account. A hearing can weigh what he has observed in his realm and the evidence brought to it; he is not a substitute for every investigation.

**6. The red city is a present place, not a replay frozen at 06:00.** It reflects the corresponding city's spaces and ordinary objects, including the returned van and keys. Its inhabitants have their own continuously advancing actions. Elias wakes from the fall before you, visits the red office, and steals your returned key ring there. There is no theft backwards in time. Do not introduce clocks or dialogue suggesting that all its contents froze when the first office worker died.

**7. Failed player attempts are noncanonical retries.** Checkpoint reloads represent another attempt at the successful story. They are not repeated resurrection experienced by the characters. Scripted deaths such as Sam drowning are canonical within that attempt. Do not add dialogue that claims the protagonist alone can repeatedly die without consequences.

**8. Survival, rescue, absolution, and release are different outcomes.** Getting someone onto the van preserves their chance to appear at judgement. It does not certify innocence, break a bargain, or guarantee release. A rescued Maya still has a contract to answer for. Derek's truthful confession likewise earns him a hearing, not automatic forgiveness.

## What actually happened

This chronology is author truth. Clues reveal it in a different order.

### Three weeks before the game

Kessler has spent years using the logistics business to move illegally obtained human remains and conceal the evidence. Avoid dialogue that depends on medically precise claims about recoverable organs from long-dead bodies. The clinic and the transport records establish the trade; the story does not need a technical explanation of transplantation.

Tomas, the night cleaner, finds identifying tags removed from bodies being moved through Silo 2. He photographs the tags and tells dispatch supervisor Maya that he intends to report it. Maya fears exposure of the cash she has taken to alter schedules. She calls Kessler and names Tomas.

Kessler orders Derek, already implicated in illegal deliveries, to kill Tomas. He uses threats against both brothers and the evidence of their work to coerce him. Derek chooses to comply. Coercion explains the choice; the story does not call it involuntary or harmless. Tomas dies on 13 April.

Derek tells the protagonist that he struck a cleaner during a confrontation. He does not admit the knife or the death. Kessler says Tomas left the building alive and threatens prosecution for assault. The protagonist rewrites the statement from an assault into a fall, then gets Derek and the other staff to sign. He knows he is falsifying an account of violence. He does not yet know he is concealing a murder.

The report is an internal workplace record, never an official finding by police. It alleges a fall followed by Tomas leaving, not a recovered body or a confirmed accidental death. The public case remains a missing-person investigation. This distinction must be consistent in the TV report, calendar, company report, and every ghost's account.

In the city, Tomas attempts to reach the bridge alone and loses his temporary body to the street Damned. He becomes a Lost soul whose strongest attachment is the place where he was murdered. His ability to appear around the depot and silo is established; he cannot simply leave in a van by himself.

### The following weeks

Derek cannot live with the murder or the altered statement. He begins collecting copies of dispatch records, identifying tags, clinic invoices, and camera material. He writes a full account naming himself, Kessler, Maya's call, and his brother's report. He also identifies which parts he witnessed and which are documented elsewhere; his confession is not inexplicable omniscience.

He arranges with Mrs. Okafor, a trusted regular customer, for a plain parcel to be collected by an independent investigator she knows. She understands that it contains serious evidence, not all its contents. The handover is planned for after the delivery, with instructions to proceed whether or not Derek calls. Derek puts it onto his brother's existing round under an ordinary customer delivery label. He tells his brother only that this one must be handed to her personally.

The clinic cool box remains an actual illegal company delivery. It is not secretly a second heroic parcel. Derek has copied the relevant shipment identifier into his evidence. Thus one stop carries the truth about the operation while the next shows the protagonist still carrying its cargo. This contrast is intentional and comprehensible after the reveal.

Maya discovers Derek accessing records but does not find the outgoing evidence parcel or know its recipient. She sends Elias copies of selected genuine material: the stabbing image, Kessler's role, and the falsified report. She conceals her own call and presents the report's signatures as proof that all signatories knowingly endorsed the murder. Elias gains facts and a disastrous interpretation, not supernatural knowledge.

Maya points Elias toward Kessler at the depot and Derek at the flat. She expects a targeted attack followed by Elias's arrest or death. She does not foresee or control the entire massacre. Sam is the mechanic whose signature was obtained under pressure; he neither arranged the murder nor knows about Maya's call.

## The playable story, scene by scene

### 1. The ordinary round

Keep the traffic, pedestrians, van handling, delivery beacons, and grandmother-on-scooter scare. Ordinary life must feel worth losing. Most dialogue here should be practical and human, not a string of ominous predictions.

At the first stop, Mrs. Okafor has a question about the parcel:

> **Mrs. Okafor:** Your brother said not to let you take it back.
>
> **You:** What did he order now?
>
> **Mrs. Okafor:** Didn't say. He wanted someone to sign for it.
>
> **You:** He worries about everything.

She signs. The delivery completes normally. The camera gives the same modest attention to her signature that it gives any handover. Do not turn it into a glowing mystery object.

At Harlow Clinic, the recipient asks for the shipment number rather than the driver's name. You notice that he removes its label before carrying it inside. He calls it routine. Your later office ledger contains that exact shipment number. The player does not have to memorize it; the protagonist recognizes it when reading the record.

On the way back, Derek's existing phone or fridge-note content establishes that he wanted breakfast together and asked you not to work an extra round. A message from you reads: **“We fixed the statement. Stop letting him scare you.”** This is an early, fair indication of your involvement. Never let the protagonist claim to have no idea what statement is being discussed.

The van goes into the garage. The keys go through the slot. The work is done.

### 2. The flat and the roof

Derek is dead in the bedroom. The camera footage is real and remains continuous around the killing. Elias is the attacker; no later reveal changes a stabbing into an attempted rescue.

Derek tries to speak before the attack. The recording gives us only **“It's already gone—”** before Elias interrupts him. On first viewing it seems to be an attempt to deny possessing something. Later it refers to the evidence parcel. Avoid implying that Derek invited or intended his death.

Elias stayed because he wanted to confront the protagonist, whom Maya's packet identifies as the author of the false report. He has been waiting, rereading Derek's phone, increasingly shaken by what he has done. When discovered, he is confronted by an enraged man rather than the controlled interrogation he imagined. He retreats through the open stairwell. A clearly visible maintenance notice establishes that the roof normally connects to the neighboring building; that escape gate has recently been chained shut. Elias reaches it and realizes he is trapped. This explains the upward route without requiring the player to solve a new puzzle.

At the roof, he says:

> **Elias:** You wrote it. “He fell.” Two words and my brother was gone.
>
> **You:** You killed Derek.
>
> **Elias:** Ask him what he did to Tomas.
>
> **You:** You don't get to say his name.

You push him. He grabs your wrist out of the instinct to survive, not as a plan to visit hell. Both fall. The camera must show an intentional shove, not retroactively excuse it as an accident.

The prologue ends with the player understanding that the protagonist is grieving and dangerous. Neither the story nor the final judgement forgets what he just did.

### 3. Waking below

Keep the red city, fists, feeding-zombie reveal, street threats, and locked van. The first objective is concrete: retrieve the keys and reach the bridge, where Derek might have gone. The office is both the place to find transport and the place that can explain Elias's accusation.

The first mandatory conversation with Priya establishes death and the office group's second deaths. One compact exchange is sufficient:

> **Priya:** We tried the road. All five of us. We thought dying was the worst thing that could happen.
>
> **You:** Where's Derek?
>
> **Priya:** He came through after us. Left you something. Said he was going to tell it properly this time.

She identifies the bridge as the place where hearings happen. She does not promise it is an open road home. The protagonist now has a reason to search for Derek and a reason he cannot simply leave the office investigation unfinished.

Priya also corrects the city's rumor: people sometimes speak and then turn, but the important words are an agreement with the voice offering them another body. She and the other office workers heard the offer after losing theirs. Kessler has been listening to it. The player later hears the same terms when he accepts.

### 4. The office: rebuild the account

Every puzzle opens a useful source of evidence or a physical route.

**The fuses** restore lights, the keypad, and the security terminal. Ghost hints are frightened accounts of where they hid the components, not a dead murderer arranging a scavenger hunt.

**The keypad** uses 1304, still derived from the calendar. It was reset on the night of the incident to keep staff out of the record room. A security notice links the reset to that date. Kessler used a memorable code because it was convenient and he believed he controlled the people around him; do not claim the simple code was an ingenious concealment. Maya had supplied Elias with access information for his original attack, so the locked room does not imply he somehow guessed it.

**The CCTV ordering puzzle** reconstructs the cleaner's route across four separate camera exports. The files have camera labels and timestamps but were removed from the terminal's normal index during the cover-up. Ordering them is rebuilding the sequence, not a magical requirement that a murder tape will play only for someone who knows a cleaner's schedule.

The ghosts supply different pieces:

- Priya knows the arrival below, the failed escape, and Derek's later visit.
- Ben remembers Tomas as a person: his habit of saving broken mugs, his plans to go home, the last shift circled on the calendar. Kindness here must concern someone, not merely select a conspicuously virtuous answer.
- Nadia witnessed the attack and Maya and Sam entering the service route.
- Omar admits preserving only part of the camera export after obeying Kessler's deletion order. His fear compromised him. He can still help.
- Tomas identifies the report as the account written about his death. He does not claim to have authored it.

On your desk are two items within the existing note interaction: Derek's statement and Elias's fresh challenge written across its cover: **“I've got your keys. Silo 2. Bring the man who wrote this.”** The handwriting and wet blood make the challenge visibly later than the statement beneath it.

Derek's account admits the stabbing and explains the parcel. Give the player the factual information here, but let the CCTV make it impossible to dismiss as a forced confession. The narration recognizes the protagonist's own part without pretending he had amnesia:

> **You:** I knew he'd hit someone. I changed that much. Kessler said the man had walked out.
>
> **Tomas:** Did you ask to see him?
>
> **You:** No.

The tape shows Derek killing Tomas. Keep the existing animation. After it, the protagonist reads the closing part of Derek's statement aloud:

> **Derek's statement:** I knew you would try to help. You always do. That's why I didn't tell you what was in the parcel. For once I needed you to leave it with somebody else.

The delivery receipt on the route sheet and Mrs. Okafor's earlier line make this verifiable to the player. Derek planned a living disclosure, not a supernatural scavenger hunt for someone he expected to die.

This is the central turn of the game. Your brother did not need you to erase the crime. He needed a way to stop being protected from it.

### 5. Kessler buys another body

The office confrontation remains mandatory. Kessler tries to restore the old hierarchy: he provided jobs, money, protection; therefore everybody owes him silence. He admits coercing Derek and then offers the protagonist the same excuse:

> **Kessler:** I kept your brother out of prison.
>
> **You:** So did I. Look where we put him instead.

The voice offering the contract is audible to the player, brief, and comprehensible. It offers a body strong enough to prevent witnesses reaching judgement and states that the body will belong to the King. Kessler explicitly accepts. His change starts on that acceptance, not on a confession or an arbitrary dialogue-tree exit.

His dropped silo key is carried with him: remove every old line about it remaining in a safe. The key is needed to open the public entrance to the locked service route. Maya and Sam fled through an internal emergency route during the attack; Elias got its access information from Maya. None needed to wait for the player to unlock this particular door.

You escape while Kessler transforms. His humanoid monster and later giant are the same soul in the same borrowed body. Further power makes it larger and less controllable. He has not been a hidden demon for forty years, and he is not killed in the office.

### 6. Tomas holds the door

The body-bag tunnel, banging door, chase, footbridge, and timed knob puzzle remain. These are the physical service routes used by the operation. The bags are evidence of what moved through them, not decorations placed by Elias for your benefit.

Tomas appears where he was last dragged toward the silo. He can hold Kessler here briefly because of that attachment. His form visibly thins as he does it. The player sees both the help and its limit.

> **You:** After what I wrote—why are you helping me?
>
> **Tomas:** Sam's still in there.

That is enough. It refuses both a forgiveness speech and another murder performed in Tomas's name.

The knob is physically jammed by the collapse, so alternating A and D forces it open while Tomas buys seconds. Failure gives Kessler an opening to hurt you. Once through, the door closes and Kessler pounds against it. Tomas withdraws as an exhausted ghost; he is not destroyed forever. Kessler eventually takes the exterior route, explaining his later emergence near the office and van.

### 7. Elias in the silo

Preserve the first ambush, forced retreat, ladder traversal, lift, second ambush, spiral stairs, jump, and nonlethal half-health loss. Elias knows these routes because Maya gave him a maintenance plan for the original attack and he inspected the depot while investigating Tomas. His apparent head start comes from waking first, not teleporting arbitrarily.

He treats the protagonist's explanations as another revised statement. He has already seen that the evidence parcel existed in Derek's note but believes it was either another lie or a private attempt to buy safety. He has not read the full copies sent to Mrs. Okafor and does not know whether she received them. His refusal is a character failure, not ignorance maintained by everyone refusing to speak.

Before the fight, Maya and Sam's dialogue establishes the collapse and bargain. Sam says they saw their own bodies below the broken walkway. He heard a voice offer another body; he refused. Maya says she only asked for protection. The protagonist can see the matching dark mark on her wrist in the existing rifle-throw close-up. The bargain's delayed collection was explained in the office; Sam's account identifies Maya as someone who accepted it.

Maya throws the rifle because she wants Elias silenced. She has been trying to control the account since she sent him the first packet. Elias still has a weapon and is coming for you, so shooting him during the fight is self-defense. Killing him after he is disarmed and beaten is a separate, optional act.

His dodges, charges, embedded knives, stun openings, and health drops remain. No lore depends on the player using a particular attack.

When he kneels, both branches first receive this exchange:

> **You:** He sent it. I delivered it myself. He put his own name in it.
>
> **Elias:** He tried to tell me something was already gone.
>
> **You:** You didn't let him finish.

Elias's recognition is not proof that he has suddenly become good. It is the first crack in the story he used to authorize his murders. Now the player chooses whether to let a defeated enemy live with the truth.

### 8A. If you execute Elias

Keep the shot, corpse, badge, photograph, and key pickup. The photograph of the brothers repeats an ordinary pose from the picture in your flat. It does not reveal Elias's identity for the first time; you already know it. Its purpose is to make the parallel personal.

Maya says, **“Now we can get out.”** The relief should be uncomfortable. She admits the bonuses and the forged report, but deflects her role in choosing Tomas and weaponizing Elias. No transformation happens because no new bargain is made or invoked.

Sam contradicts her before the flood:

> **Sam:** You gave him the names. I saw you send them.
>
> **Maya:** I gave him Kessler. I didn't tell him to kill everyone.

Thus executing Elias does not remove the Maya revelation. Derek's written evidence independently records her original call; Sam establishes her later manipulation. The player can save her with full knowledge that she is culpable.

Taking the keys opens the route toward escape. Movement and violence in the damaged shaft have compromised the old disposal sluice; establish cracks and rising pressure before the choice. It fails now in both branches. The flood is the red city's physical accumulation of the operation's victims, pouring through its disposal system. It is not divine punishment specifically triggered by mercy or execution.

Maya and Sam can both cling to you, drown, survive, join, and board the van exactly as they do now.

### 8B. If you spare Elias

Elias starts to identify Maya as the source of his packet. Sam confirms it. Faced with two surviving witnesses and the fact that Derek's parcel is beyond her reach, Maya drops the pretense that there is a private deal to be made.

> **Maya:** I got you past the gate. I gave you the man who did it. You were supposed to stop there.
>
> **Elias:** You gave him Tomas first.

She sees that Elias has lost his appetite for the story she sold him. She decides to take the power she reserved. Her unmistakable invocation is **“I accepted your price. Give it to me now.”** Her wrist mark burns before the transformation. No one says that admitting a secret caused it.

Keep her descent, attack, and Elias's intervention. Elias cannot undo his murders; he can stop her from committing another one. He turns from the player to the immediate threat, throws the keys, and holds her while the blood rises.

> **Elias:** Tomas wanted somebody to listen. I didn't listen either. Go.

Maya remains bound. Elias has made no bargain. When their bodies are lost in the flood, she remains Damned and he becomes a Lost soul. The player does not recruit him. A brief human voice from the shaft, or a later line from the King, confirms that his sacrifice cost his body rather than erasing him from existence. His case remains unresolved inside the city.

Only Sam can be rescued in this branch. Maya chose her transformation; it is not counted as a companion the player failed to pull out.

### 9. The flood and the decision to bring people

Keep the Space-kick endurance, air loss, buoyancy, companions' grip, drowning, and climb-out. The scene tests the player's ability under pressure. Do not automatically describe a drowned companion as someone the player deliberately murdered or abandoned.

Sam's drowning leaves a blue Lost manifestation. Maya's drowning collects the bargain she previously accepted, causing the existing hostile return on the stairs. The same rule produced Kessler's body and her voluntary mercy-branch transformation.

At the top, use the existing interaction to make recruitment explicit:

> **Prompt:** Bring Sam to the van.
>
> **Prompt:** Bring Maya to judgement.

Maya may ask whether you will really turn her over. The protagonist should not promise forgiveness in exchange for following. **“I'm getting you there alive enough to answer.”** This is what distinguishes rescuing her from protecting Derek through a lie.

Anyone left alive stays at the silo refuge. They are not silently declared dead. Anyone drowned remains below in their established form. The final verdict must name the actual outcome.

### 10. The last delivery

The van is the same vehicle used in the prologue. The driver now knows the people it carries. Brief voices from the rear during the escape make their presence tangible without introducing a passenger-management mechanic.

Kessler returns near the office. He has been unable to force his way directly into the silo and has taken the exterior service route. He draws more power into the body he bought. His giant form exposes the people he has treated as property; it is not a second, previously unknown villain.

He wants to kill these witnesses before they reach the bridge. He is trying to preserve control, not undo the living parcel, whose destination he does not know and cannot reach from death.

> **Kessler:** Everything in that van belongs to me.
>
> **Sam, if aboard:** I worked for him eighteen years. He never learned my surname.

Keep the walking horde, clingers, swerving, drive-by rifle, lock-on, road cameras, bonnet camera in the tunnel, and Kessler's roof detour. He cannot fit inside the tunnel in his giant body, so the same physical limitation explains his route over it.

The horde stops at the bridge because it fears the place of judgement. There is no absolute barrier excluding every Damned creature. Kessler crosses because his desperation and rage outweigh his fear. A line establishes this distinction before the arena.

### 11. The bridge fight

The vehicle remains usable. Drive, ram, shoot the heart, dodge the orbs, and survive the existing attack phases. If the van is wrecked, the fight continues on foot. Do not add a mandatory destruction cutscene that contradicts the actual game.

Before the arena closes, any companions aboard escape into a sheltered stair recess on the approach side of the bridge. Use a short camera beat and reused character models, or an unmistakable sound and line if animation scope is limited. Their rescue state persists even if the van later explodes. They return for judgement after the fight.

Each phase strips Kessler's claim of ownership further. The people he summons are the dead he profited from. He can force their damaged bodies to move while his borrowed heart holds them, but their resistance is visible: hands pull at him between attacks. This foreshadows why they drag him under once the heart fails. They did not inexplicably switch allegiance at zero health.

His final appeal is to the protagonist's old excuse:

> **Kessler:** You would have done anything for your brother.
>
> **You:** I did the wrong thing for him. That's mine. You don't get to use it anymore.

The drowned pull his borrowed body into the water. Kessler is taken into the King's custody. Killing a boss body is not proof that its soul ceased to exist or escaped judgement.

### 12. The hearing

The King rises using the existing staging. His name, voice, and authority have been foreshadowed since the office. His arrival resolves a known destination rather than introducing the story's rules in its final minutes.

He states that Derek came of his own accord, gave the complete account, and received a sentence beyond the bridge. That is not paradise or resurrection. The protagonist asks whether he can follow. The King answers that brothers do not share a verdict.

Let the emotional resolution come from Derek's statement, already read, with a short final passage in the protagonist's voice:

> **Derek's statement:** Don't tell them I was a good man. Tell them what I did. You can still tell them I was your brother.

The protagonist says Derek killed Tomas, Elias killed Derek, and he himself falsified the report and pushed Elias from the roof. He does not ask the King to replace those facts with one convenient villain.

The King distinguishes the fixed past from the later choices. The rooftop killing counts as a real crime shared by every playthrough. The ending variables measure what the protagonist did after learning the fuller truth; they do not pretend the prologue offered a choice it did not have.

Every rescued companion is addressed on camera or by name and an audible response. Sam is granted a separate hearing beyond the fog, not instant exoneration. Maya's protection contract is called in at her hearing and she is taken into custody; getting her here prevented another anonymous death, not the consequences of her bargain. If she was left alive, drowned, or transformed in the silo, say that instead. Tomas and a spared Elias remain Lost inside the city; their unresolved cases give restitution a concrete beginning.

**Damned ending.** The King cites the actual pattern: execution of a defeated man, cruelty, and people who did not reach the bridge. For accidental drowning he says “You could not bring him through,” not “You chose his death.” The city is severe, but the narration must not invent intent. The protagonist is sentenced to a hunter's body and walks back into the city. Any passengers already delivered remain entitled to their own hearings; they are not punished again because their driver failed his.

**Restitution ending.** The King does not praise the protagonist as innocent. He sentences him to preserve the accounts he once falsified: return, locate the Lost, learn their names, and bring truthful testimony to the bridge. The mark burns into his hands. The role may retain the game's term “confessor,” but it means a witness who hears complete accounts, not someone who makes people transform by tricking them into speech. Hostile Damned may need to be subdued; innocent ghosts never need to be killed to fill a quota.

The road beyond remains closed to the protagonist until his sentence is served. The chapter closes on the resolved truth about both brothers, Kessler's defeat, the fate of the delivered companions, and the protagonist's own sentence. “TO BE CONTINUED” introduces that new work; it does not substitute for this chapter's ending.

## Fair clues before the revelations

Every important revelation needs an earlier observable fact. The protagonist may misunderstand evidence; the camera and documents should not lie to the player.

| Early detail | First interpretation | Later payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Derek insists the parcel must reach Mrs. Okafor, with no return to the depot. | A personal favour during a normal shift. | He is sending evidence beyond Kessler's control. |
| The protagonist recognises an unusual correction on the accident report. | Familiar office paperwork. | He wrote the false account himself. Acknowledge recognition immediately; reveal its full consequences later. |
| The clinic delivery matches a dispatch entry in the office ledger. | An unpleasant but ordinary job. | The player's work physically connects the business to the crime. |
| Tomas remembers being threatened and his brother's name. | A frightened ghost needs help. | The victim is Elias's brother. His existence also disproves any rule equating ghosthood with guilt. |
| A dated statement appears in Derek's file; Maya avoids discussing its date. | Maya is frightened of reopening the case. | Derek had begun confessing before Maya directed Elias to him. |
| Maya repeats a phrase about keeping her body, and refuses to explain who offered it. | Panic about another death. | She has already reserved the King's protection. |
| Kessler names a price before changing. | A desperate negotiation. | Acceptance, not confession, activates the monstrous body. |
| An early notice describes where arrivals can seek a hearing; Priya saw Derek leave toward it. | World orientation. | Derek's absence has a destination and a resolution. |
| Elias carries the keys; the emptied deposit has a later timestamp than the fall. | He has been ahead of the player. | He stole the keys after arriving. No impossible pre-death theft is needed. |

Mandatory clues belong on the critical path or in required conversations. Optional notes can deepen motive, but must not contain the only explanation of a necessary rule. Repeat essential rules through consequences as well as words.

## Ending logic: preserve the actual game rules

Keep the existing judgement arithmetic. Executing Elias adds one; sparing him subtracts one. Kind karma (at least +2) subtracts one; cruel karma (at most −2) adds one; intermediate karma adds nothing. Each eligible companion subtracts one if rescued and adds one otherwise. A companion already marked **Turned** is excluded. A positive total gives **Damned**; zero or negative gives **Confessor / restitution**.

| Elias choice | Eligible rescue result | Kind karma | Neutral karma | Cruel karma |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kill | Maya and Sam rescued | Restitution | Restitution | Restitution |
| Kill | Exactly one rescued | Restitution | Damned | Damned |
| Kill | Neither rescued | Damned | Damned | Damned |
| Spare | Sam rescued; Maya Turned and excluded | Restitution | Restitution | Restitution |
| Spare | Sam not rescued; Maya Turned and excluded | Restitution | Restitution | Damned |

This is the game's judgement model, not an assertion that killing one person can ethically be cancelled by saving another. Write the King as judging the whole pattern; he must still name wrongdoing on the restitution path. Do not claim that mercy guarantees a particular ending, or that executing Elias guarantees damnation.

The speech must distinguish deliberate abandonment from a failed rescue. The score can stay unchanged while the narration accurately states what happened. Likewise, **Drowned**, **Turned**, **Rescued**, and left alive are different character states even where the score treats two states alike.

## Literary research and how it informs this original plot

No one can honestly claim to have reviewed all great novels and stories for one rewrite. This treatment draws on a focused selection of primary texts and applies structural principles rather than borrowing their characters or plots.

- **Aristotle, *Poetics*:** recognition and reversal should emerge from the preceding action. The parcel, altered report, and reserved bargain make the surprises causal and retrospectively legible. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1974/1974-h/1974-h.htm)
- **Edgar Allan Poe, “The Philosophy of Composition”:** design toward a deliberate final effect. Carrying the first parcel and finally carrying truthful testimony give the beginning and ending a shared action with changed meaning. [Primary text](https://www.eapoe.org/works/essays/philcomp.htm)
- **Robert Louis Stevenson, *Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*:** a late account can transform the reader's understanding of earlier evidence. Derek's confession revises his intentions without erasing his responsibility. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/42/42-h/42-h.htm)
- **Fyodor Dostoyevsky, *Crime and Punishment*:** admitting a crime and undergoing moral change are different stages. The restitution ending begins difficult work instead of awarding instant innocence. [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2554/2554-h/2554-h.htm)
- **W. W. Jacobs, “The Monkey's Paw”:** a supernatural promise becomes frightening through its consequences. Maya receives the bodily protection promised to her; its ownership clause makes the fulfilment horrific. [Primary collection](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12122/pg12122-images.html)

The governing discipline is that every twist changes the meaning of an established action. None depends on declaring the game a dream, replacing the visible killer, adding an unseen mastermind at the end, or withholding something the protagonist would naturally acknowledge.
